### The money that dies on the shelf

*Insider · 1 September 2026 · 4 min read · https://ronipaul.com/notes/the-money-that-dies-on-the-shelf*

**A meaningful slice of everything an Indian pharmacy buys never sells. It expires. Nobody tracks it in real time, so nobody catches it until it is already a loss.**

Walk into any neighbourhood pharmacy in India and you are looking at a business quietly bleeding money. Most owners cannot see where. It is not on the counter and it is not in the register. It is on the shelf, in the third row from the back, in a strip that expired last Tuesday.

I grew up watching this. My father and uncle run a pharmacy, a drug-distribution operation and a diagnostic centre, so I learnt how a chemist buys, stocks, prices and writes off stock long before I understood anything else about business. The write-off was always the quiet number. Nobody said it out loud. It just arrived at the end of the quarter like weather.

#### How the loss actually happens

A medical shop carries somewhere between three and eight thousand distinct products. Each one has a batch and an expiry. Each one was bought from one of several distributors, on credit, at a margin that is thin to begin with. When the shop is busy, which is most of the time, the person at the counter is dispensing, not auditing. Stock lives in someone's head.

So a slow-moving strip sits. Nobody looks at it until a customer asks for it, and by then it has three weeks left and cannot be sold. It goes into the expiry box. Sometimes the distributor takes it back at a discount. Often it does not. Either way the money that bought it is gone, and the only place it shows up is in a smaller number at the end of the year.

> It is not a discipline problem. It is a tooling problem.

The owners I sit with are not careless. They are running today's volumes on yesterday's systems: a billing PC from a decade ago, a register, and memory. Ask any one of them what expires next month and they will tell you honestly that they do not know. Not because they do not care. Because nothing they own can tell them.

#### What a real fix looks like

The fix is not a warning label on the shelf. It is a system that knows, at the moment a strip is billed in, when it will expire, and that surfaces the answer before it matters: a list every Monday of what to push, what to return and what to stop ordering. Batch-level stock. Expiry sorted by date, not by product. Reorder suggestions built from what actually sells in this shop, not a national average.

That is a boring sentence and it is the whole opportunity. India has more than twelve lakh chemists and druggists. If even a modest share of their purchases is dying on the shelf, the money lost every year across the country is not small. It is one of the largest untouched pools of margin in Indian retail, and it belongs to some of the hardest-working small businesses in the country.

This is the first thing MedMate does for a shop, and it is free, because a pharmacy that can see its own stock is a pharmacy that survives long enough to do everything else.

### Why I left medical school

*Human · 5 September 2026 · 4 min read · https://ronipaul.com/notes/why-i-left-medical-school*

**Not because it was hard. Because the thing failing patients was never the medicine. It was everything around it.**

I dropped out of medical school. It took me years to say that sentence without adding a defence to the end of it. Now I think it was the best decision I have made, and I want to explain why, because it is not the reason people assume.

I did not leave because it was hard. Medicine is hard in a way I found satisfying. I left because I kept noticing the same thing, on the wards, in the clinics, in the pharmacy at home: the thing failing patients was rarely the medicine. It was everything around it. The access. The wait. The prescription that was written and never filled because the shop was closed and the app said out of stock. The systems people had to fight through before care ever reached them.

#### A pharmacy family

I was raised behind a counter. My father and uncle run a pharmacy, a drug-distribution business and a diagnostic centre. I have seen the good days, when the shop was full and everyone in the area knew us by name, and the tough days, when stock got stuck, money got stuck, and the whole family carried the worry home. So the gap between what medicine can do and what actually reaches a family was not an abstract idea for me. It was Sunday morning at home.

Somewhere in medical school I realised I did not want to be one more person inside that system. I wanted to build the parts around it that were missing. That is a different job. It needed a different training.

#### Seven years, three companies

That was around 2019. Since then I have built two companies. The first was in hospitality, launched as Covid arrived; the pandemic hit that industry twice and it could not absorb the second blow. I moved to Bengaluru and joined a healthcare startup as Chief Operating Officer, working with hospitals and as a partner in the business, to learn how technology companies actually ship. Then I founded a media agency, which I still run.

> The dropout is a detail. The record is the claim.

I say all of that not to dress up a decision but because it is the honest sequence. Leaving medicine did not make me a founder. Seven years of building, failing once, and building again did. MedMate is my third company, and it is the one I have been preparing for my whole life, because it is the business I grew up inside.

If you are in a degree you know is not yours, I will not tell you to leave. I will tell you that the world outside has its own exams, they are harder, and nobody grades on a curve. Leave for something, not from something. I left for this.

### The customer who moved

*Visionary · 9 September 2026 · 5 min read · https://ronipaul.com/notes/the-customer-who-moved*

**The customer didn't leave the chemist. They moved. The restaurant went online, the kirana went online. Why should the medical shop be the one left out?**

Every pharmacy owner I meet in Bengaluru says a version of the same sentence. Sales are down. Customers are down. And I do not know what I did wrong.

You did nothing wrong. The game changed, and nobody came and told you the new rules.

#### What actually happened

Your customer did not leave you. They moved. They still trust the shop that has known their family for twenty years and knows their mother's prescription by heart. They simply stopped walking to it. They are sitting at home now, they open an app, they order, and it comes to their door in ten minutes from a warehouse that does not know their name.

The big platforms solved delivery. They solved it only for their own warehouses. They spent crores on riders, dark stores and discounts, and they were never going to spend a rupee of it on the corner chemist. So the same customer you have served for two decades is now buying from a company that has never met them, not because the company is better, but because it is closer to their couch.

> The customer went online. The shop got left behind. Alone.

#### The unfair part

A restaurant did not have to build Swiggy to be on Swiggy. A kirana did not have to build a logistics network to be on a quick-commerce app. But a pharmacy that wants to reach the customer at home has needed its own app, its own rider, its own software, and its own money to pay for all three. No single shop can do that. So they did not, and the giants moved in.

That is the gap MedMate exists to close. Your shop goes live on the app. People in your area open it and see your name, your stock, your prices. They order from you. A rider picks it up from your counter and takes it to their door. You do not need an app. You do not need a delivery boy. We built that part so you do not have to. And the customer stays yours, because they are ordering from the shop they always trusted, just from home now.

#### Why the pharmacy is different

I am often asked why quick commerce cannot simply absorb medicine the way it absorbed groceries. Because medicine is not groceries. It needs a prescription checked by a pharmacist who is accountable for it. It needs a cold chain for insulin. It needs someone who will notice that this month's dose is different from last month's and ask why. A warehouse is not built for any of that. A neighbourhood chemist has done all of it, quietly, for thirty years.

So the answer was never one more faceless pharmacy app. It was giving the corner shop the same technology the giants had, and connecting it to the families living a few streets away. Namma means ours. Our shops, our streets, our fight. The restaurant went online. The kirana went online. It is the medical shop's turn.

### Twelve lakh counters

*Insider · 31 October 2026 · 5 min read · https://ronipaul.com/notes/twelve-lakh-counters*

**India has more medicine counters than post offices and government hospitals put together. Almost none of them are online. That gap is the whole opportunity.**

Every founder deck about Indian healthcare has a slide with a big number on it. For years mine said nine lakh pharmacies. I had seen it in a dozen articles and never once seen where it came from. When we finally went looking, the trail went cold. So we threw it out and started from the people who actually count.

#### The number, and who counts it

The All India Organisation of Chemists & Druggists, the trade body that every licensed chemist in the country belongs to, put the figure at 12.40 lakh chemists and druggists in March 2026. Twelve lakh forty thousand. One point two four million shops with a drug licence, a pharmacist's name on the board and a shutter that goes up every morning.

That is the number I use now, and the only one I will put my name to, because the source is the shops themselves.

#### What twelve lakh looks like

Numbers that big stop meaning anything, so put it next to things you already know.

India Post, the largest postal network in the world, ran 1,64,999 post offices at the end of March 2025. There are about seven and a half medicine counters for every post office.

The Central Bureau of Health Intelligence counted 36,626 government hospitals at the end of 2022. There are about 34 chemists for every one of them.

Put every post office and every government hospital in India side by side and the chemists still outnumber them six to one. The medicine shop is not part of India's healthcare network. In most towns, for most days of the year, it is the network. It is where the fever is first described, where the old man's monthly strip is kept aside, where the prescription from the clinic actually becomes a tablet in a hand.

> India's largest healthcare network has no head office. It has twelve lakh shutters.

#### The part of the number nobody says out loud

Here is the line that should be on the slide next to the big number: almost none of those twelve lakh counters can take an order from a phone or send a rider to a door.

That is not a criticism of the chemists. Building an app, hiring riders and running software is a crore-rupee problem, and no single shop was ever going to solve it alone. So the apps arrived, solved delivery for their own warehouses, and started pulling customers out of shops that had served them for twenty years. On 20 May 2026 chemists across the country downed shutters for a day in protest against deep discounting by online platforms. Twelve lakh counters, and the only way they had been able to make themselves heard was by closing.

#### Why I read the number differently

Most people see twelve lakh pharmacies and think fragmentation: too many small shops, too hard to reach, better to build a few big warehouses and deliver around them.

I grew up behind one of those counters, so I see the opposite. Twelve lakh locations already stocked, already licensed, already trusted, already within walking distance of almost every household in the country. Nobody will ever build a warehouse network that dense. It exists. It is just offline.

The job is not to replace the network. It is to connect it. Give the counter an online storefront, a rider when it needs one, software that tracks what expires next month, and a prescription that arrives from the clinic already checked. Do that once, and the thing you have built is not one more pharmacy app. It is India's medicine network, finally reachable from a phone.

That is what MedMate is for. And it is why, when someone asks me how big the market is, I no longer reach for a revenue figure. I say twelve lakh counters, and then I ask how many of them they can see from where they are standing.

### Online without an app

*Builder · 31 October 2026 · 5 min read · https://ronipaul.com/notes/online-without-an-app*

**Every chemist I meet asks the same question: do I need my own app now? No. The restaurant never built one either. Here is what going online actually takes, and who should carry which part.**

Every chemist I sit with gets to the same question within ten minutes. "So I need my own app now?"

It is the wrong question, and it is not the chemist's fault. For five years the only version of "online" they have seen is an app with a big brand on it and a warehouse behind it. So they assume that to go online they must become that. Build an app. Hire a delivery boy. Learn software. Most look at the cost and the headache and decide, reasonably, to wait. And while they wait, the customer who has walked to their counter for twenty years quietly starts ordering from a phone.

#### What "online" actually is

Strip the word down and selling medicine online is seven separate jobs.

A storefront people can find. A stock list that is true right now, not last Tuesday. A way to take the order. A registered pharmacist who checks the prescription before anything is packed, because Schedule H medicines cannot legally go out without one. A rider to carry it. Payment that settles cleanly. And a path for the strip that comes back.

The shop already does three of those seven every day: it holds the licence, it holds the stock, it has the pharmacist. The other four are technology and logistics, and they are expensive precisely because they only make sense at scale. A rider sitting idle costs the same whether the shop has three orders or thirty. An app costs the same to maintain for one shop as for a thousand. That is why no single chemist was ever going to do this alone, and why twelve lakh of them have mostly not.

#### The restaurant did not build an app

Here is the thing nobody says to the chemist. The restaurant on the corner never built an app. The kirana never hired a software team. They went online by joining something that already had the customers, the riders and the code, and they put their own name on the listing. The hard four jobs moved to the platform. The shop kept doing what it was good at.

Pharmacies got left out of that wave for one honest reason: medicine is not food. There is a prescription to check and a licence on the line. The platforms that scaled fastest solved it by building their own warehouses with their own pharmacists, which meant the neighbourhood counter was not a partner but a competitor. On 20 May 2026 chemists across India shut their shutters for a day to say exactly that.

> The chemist does not need to become an app. The chemist needs an app that works for the chemist.

#### How it works when the shop stays the shop

This is the shape of it on MedMate, and it is deliberately boring.

The shop goes live under its own name. People nearby open the app and see that shop, its stock and its prices. They order. The order lands on the pharmacist's screen, and nothing is packed until the pharmacist has checked the prescription, the same check that happens at the counter today. A MedMate rider picks it up and takes it to the door in minutes. The billing, the stock, the expiry tracking and the order history all run in the same software the shop uses for walk-ins, so there is no second system to learn.

What the shop supplies: the licence, the stock, the pharmacist and the trust. What the platform supplies: the storefront, the orders, the rider and the software. The set-up happens at the shop, with the staff, in a day. If it took longer than that, nobody would touch it, and I would not either.

#### The objection that matters

The sharp chemists do not ask about technology. They ask who owns the customer.

On MedMate the answer is the shop. The customer sees the shop's name, orders from that shop and is served by that pharmacist. The platform does not sit between the chemist and the family; it carries the bag. That is the whole difference between being on a platform and being replaced by one, and it is the line I will not move.

So, to the question. No, you do not need your own app. You need the four jobs you cannot do alone to be done for you, under your name, at a price that makes sense for a shop and not a warehouse. That is what we are building. The rest you already have.
